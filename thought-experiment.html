How to interpret the financial comparisons

The study compares coverage and payment rules under four scenarios:

AExisting coverage
Existing prices
BUniversal coverage
Existing prices
CExisting coverage
Regulated prices
DUniversal coverage
Regulated prices

D minus A is the principal spending comparison. A positive value means higher modeled national healthcare spending than business as usual; a negative value means lower modeled spending. These are all-payer national totals, not estimates of additional federal borrowing.

Current conclusion: The central model is more expensive than business as usual over both 30 and 100 years. Some stronger price-setting illustrations reverse the sign, but their reimbursement assumptions and real-world feasibility remain unverified. The research supports serious policy development and independent testing—not an immediate national launch.

Reading the findings responsibly

The model is a question, not a verdict.

What the research examines

  • Universal healthcare coverage and public-payer arrangements.
  • Independent healthcare providers operating within regulated payment structures.
  • Provider reimbursement, staffed capacity, financing, and long-run cost behavior.
  • Potential health and delivery outcomes requiring empirical verification.

What the financial analysis does not establish

The central model is not projected to be cheaper than business as usual across the studied 30-year and 100-year horizons. Lower-cost variants depend on more aggressive price assumptions that have not been validated as achievable.

These are scenario analyses—not demonstrated national savings, implementation guarantees, or a completed proof of policy feasibility.
